## PAGE 4 · THE FIVE PILLARS OF GREAT WEDDING MUSIC

Before you pick a single song, internalize these five principles. Every decision in this guide flows from them.

**01 · The music carries the emotion the visuals can't.**
A bride walking down the aisle is a beautiful image. A bride walking down the aisle to the right song is a memory that lives in everyone's chest for thirty years. Don't outsource that decision.

**02 · Live is not the same as loud.**
The best wedding music is mixed for the room you're in, not the size of the speaker. Live guitar at conversational volume during cocktail hour will outperform a louder playlist every single time.

**03 · Plan the transitions, not just the songs.**
What plays *between* the processional and the vows? Between dinner and the dance floor? The transitions are where amateur planning shows.

**04 · The "do-not-play" list matters more than the "must-play" list.**
You'll hear "no Cha Cha Slide" or "absolutely no Macarena" from couples we've worked with. The exclusions reveal taste faster than the inclusions.

**05 · Your musician is also your timekeeper.**
A great wedding musician knows that when the processional song ends, the ceremony begins. When the cocktail-hour set closes, dinner has been ready for ten minutes. Music is the metronome of the day.

## PAGE 5 · PART I — CEREMONY

# The Anatomy of Ceremony Music

A wedding ceremony has **four musical movements**. Most couples plan one of them well, two of them poorly, and forget the fourth entirely.

### MOVEMENT 1 — THE PRELUDE (15–30 minutes)

This is the music guests hear as they arrive, find seats, hug each other, settle in. It runs from the first guest's arrival until the moment everyone stands for the processional.

**The goal of the prelude:** create *anticipation* — not silence, not background noise. Music that quiets a room, lifts the atmosphere, and signals that something meaningful is about to begin.

**What works:**
- Solo classical guitar (timeless, never wrong)
- Solo violin or cello (gravitas, slightly more formal)
- String trio or quartet (luxe but expensive)
- Acoustic piano (if the venue has one — never rent)

**What doesn't work:**
- Anything with vocals (pulls attention)
- Pop or radio music (breaks the spell)
- Loud or rhythmic (creates restlessness)

**Sigano Music tip:** if you're hiring a guitarist or pianist for cocktail hour anyway, the prelude is usually free or near-free to add. Most musicians will play 20 minutes before the ceremony for a token fee — and it elevates the entire arrival experience.

---

## PAGE 6 · PART I — CEREMONY (continued)

### MOVEMENT 2 — THE PROCESSIONAL (5–8 minutes)

The walk down the aisle. Multiple sub-movements:

**2a — Seating of the families** (parents, grandparents)
**2b — Officiant + groom + groomsmen entrance**
**2c — Bridesmaids' entrance**
**2d — Flower girl / ring bearer**
**2e — Bride's entrance**

You have two real options here:

**Option A — One continuous song that builds**
A single piece that starts gently for family seating, swells through the wedding party, and peaks at the bride. *Canon in D* (Pachelbel) is the classic choice for a reason — it's literally written to build. *Marry You* (instrumental, Bruno Mars), *A Thousand Years* (Christina Perri, slow guitar arrangement), and *Clair de Lune* (Debussy) all work.

**Option B — Distinct songs for each entrance**
More common for modern weddings. The family enters to one song, the wedding party to a second, the bride to a third. This creates **three emotional crescendos instead of one** and makes the bride's entrance hit ten times harder.

**The bride's song — three questions to ask yourself:**
1. *Is it slow enough to walk to?* (Most pop songs are faster than you think. Walk to it in your living room before deciding.)
2. *Is the meaningful lyric in the first 90 seconds?* (The aisle is short. The first minute of the song is what guests hear.)
3. *Will you want to hear it on your tenth anniversary?* (Some songs date badly. Test it by playing the song from 5 years ago you thought you'd love forever.)

---

## PAGE 7 · PART I — CEREMONY (continued)

### MOVEMENT 3 — THE INTERLUDE (during the ceremony itself, 0–5 minutes)

This is the most-forgotten ceremony moment, and often the most beautiful one. Common interlude moments:

- During the unity candle / sand / wine ritual
- During communion (if religious)
- During the seating after the kiss
- During a cultural ritual (rope/lazo, Jewish ketubah signing, hand-fasting, etc.)

A short live instrumental — even 90 seconds — turns a moment that would otherwise be awkward silence into a *cinematic pause.* If you have a non-traditional ritual in your ceremony, plan music for it.

### MOVEMENT 4 — THE RECESSIONAL + POSTLUDE (3–5 minutes)

The recessional is the walk-out. The postlude is the music while guests stand, hug, and exit to cocktail hour.

The recessional song should be **fast, joyful, and 30% louder than everything that came before.** This is the release. The emotional permission for guests to clap, cheer, and exhale.

**Classic recessional choices:**
- *Signed, Sealed, Delivered* (Stevie Wonder)
- *Marry You* (Bruno Mars)
- *You Are the Best Thing* (Ray LaMontagne)
- *Pharaon* (Gipsy Kings) — for a Spanish/Latin-inflected wedding
- *Wedding March* (Mendelssohn) — for a classic statement

**The postlude is your bridge to cocktail hour.** Most couples skip planning this and end up with a ten-second silence after the recessional ends, followed by the venue's intercom mumbling about cocktail hour. Plan three to five minutes of upbeat instrumental music to cover the gap between the kiss and the first drink.

## PAGE 9 · CEREMONY — CUSTOM ARRANGEMENTS

**Yes, you can have a custom arrangement of any song.**

A good wedding musician will take your favorite contemporary song — *Yellow* by Coldplay, *Iris* by the Goo Goo Dolls, *Perfect* by Ed Sheeran, *La Vie en Rose* by Édith Piaf, anything — and arrange it for solo guitar (or whatever instrumentation you've hired).

**What to ask for:**
- A custom arrangement of your processional song
- A custom arrangement of your bride's entrance song
- A custom arrangement of your recessional song

**What to expect:**
- A great musician will deliver a rough recording or video of the arrangement *2–4 weeks before the wedding* so you can hear it before the day
- Custom arrangements should be included free in any premium wedding-music package
- If your musician charges extra for arrangements or refuses to do them, that's a yellow flag

**Sigano Music tip:** the *bride's entrance* is the one song that absolutely must be a custom arrangement, even if everything else is standard. The version your guests hear should be the version you've already heard, loved, and signed off on.

## PAGE 10 · PART II — COCKTAIL HOUR

# The Most Underrated Hour of the Day

Most couples spend 90% of their music budget on the ceremony and reception, and treat cocktail hour as an afterthought — usually with the venue's house playlist or a spotify mix on a portable speaker.

This is the single biggest music mistake we see.

**Why cocktail hour matters more than couples realize:**

- Guests arrive from the ceremony emotional, sometimes teary, in need of *transition.* Music does that work.
- Photographers consistently say they get their best candid coverage during cocktail hour. The room's energy decides what those photos look like.
- The cocktail-hour vibe is what guests judge the wedding's "feel" by. Your reception can be incredible — but if cocktail hour was awkward, the memory of the wedding will be awkward.
- It's the only sustained hour where guests can actually *talk* to each other. The music should support that, not compete with it.

**The single principle that separates great cocktail-hour music from forgettable cocktail-hour music: *volume tuned to conversation.***

The right volume is one where two guests standing next to each other can talk without leaning in, but you can *just barely* hear the music carry across the room. That's the sweet spot. Live musicians get this right by ear. Playlists rarely do.

---

## PAGE 11 · COCKTAIL HOUR — LIVE VS. DJ VS. PLAYLIST

The honest comparison.

### LIVE MUSIC (guitar, jazz trio, string duo)

**Best for:** every cocktail hour you can afford it.

**Why:** a live musician adjusts in real time — louder when the room fills, softer when conversation peaks, faster when energy lags. A playlist can't read the room. A DJ playing recorded music *can* — but at a luxury wedding, the visual of a DJ booth during cocktail hour is wrong. Live music is *invisible production.*

**Cost:** $1,200–$2,500 for a 60–90 minute set in Miami.

### DJ ON A LAPTOP

**Best for:** if you're already paying a DJ for the reception, ask them to handle cocktail hour at a discount.

**Why:** seamless transition into the reception. Same person, same sound system, just a different playlist.

**Cost:** typically $300–600 add-on to the reception DJ package.

### PLAYLIST FROM A BLUETOOTH SPEAKER

**Best for:** backyard weddings under 40 guests, where intimacy outranks polish.

**Why:** it's free, and it works. *But* it requires someone — a friend, a family member — to babysit the speaker. The most common failure mode at small weddings is the playlist ending and no one noticing for fifteen minutes.

**Cost:** $0 (plus the cost of having a friend not enjoy the wedding).

**The Sigano Music take:** if your wedding is over 60 guests or in a luxury venue, live music for cocktail hour is non-negotiable. The cost is small relative to the wedding budget. The impact is enormous.

## PAGE 12 · COCKTAIL HOUR — SAMPLE SET LISTS BY MOOD

Hand any of these to your musician as a starting point. They'll curate around your venue, season, and crowd.

### MEDITERRANEAN / SPANISH

*For garden weddings, courtyard venues, anywhere with a "European villa" feel*

- Spanish classical guitar (Albéniz, Granados, Tárrega)
- Flamenco-inflected pieces (Sabicas, Paco de Lucía)
- Bossa nova standards (*Girl from Ipanema*, *Águas de Março*)
- Spanish guitar reinterpretations of pop (*La Vie en Rose*, *Perfect*, *Tu Me Acostumbraste*)
- Gipsy Kings repertoire (*Bamboleo*, *Volare*, *Pharaon*)

### LATIN

*For Miami weddings of any kind, especially with bicultural guest lists*

- Romantic bolero (Luis Miguel, Pedro Infante)
- Tropical at low volume (Buena Vista Social Club, Compay Segundo)
- Brazilian standards (Tom Jobim, João Gilberto)
- Modern Latin pop slowed for guitar (Romeo Santos, Juanes, Jesse & Joy)

### MODERN / TIMELESS

*For cross-cultural weddings, beach venues, anywhere "elegant but not stuffy"*

- The Great American Songbook (Sinatra, Cole Porter, Gershwin)
- Modern jazz standards (Norah Jones, Diana Krall)
- Contemporary pop reinterpreted for guitar (Coldplay, John Mayer, Adele)
- Indie folk (Vance Joy, Fleet Foxes, José González)

### CINEMATIC / DRAMATIC

*For black-tie weddings, ballroom venues, anywhere that needs gravitas*

- Film score arrangements (Hans Zimmer, Ennio Morricone)
- Classical adagios (Albinoni, Barber)
- Tango (Astor Piazzolla)
- Operatic crossover (Andrea Bocelli arrangements)

**Pick one mood. Don't mix four.** A cocktail hour with thematic coherence feels like a *world.* A cocktail hour that jumps between Spanish guitar and Sinatra and Coldplay feels like a Spotify algorithm.

## PAGE 14 · PART III — RECEPTION

# The Seven Musical Moments

Every wedding reception has seven distinct musical moments. Plan all seven. Most couples plan three.

**01 — Grand entrance** *(the couple's introduction into the reception)*
**02 — First dance** *(the couple alone on the floor)*
**03 — Parent dances** *(father-daughter, mother-son, or chosen alternatives)*
**04 — Toasts** *(speeches with background music underneath)*
**05 — Dinner** *(ambient music while everyone eats)*
**06 — Dance floor opening** *(the single most important transition of the night)*
**07 — Last dance** *(the song that ends the wedding)*

The next four pages break down each one.

---

## PAGE 15 · RECEPTION — ENTRANCE & FIRST DANCE

### MOMENT 01 — GRAND ENTRANCE (1–3 minutes)

The wedding party + couple are announced into the reception room. The energy of this moment sets the tone for the entire night.

**Two formats:**

**Format A — Wedding party first, couple last.** Each pair of the wedding party gets 8 seconds of song, the couple gets 30 seconds of crescendo, then a hard cut into the first dance.

**Format B — Couple only.** Cleaner, faster, more elegant. The wedding party is already seated. The couple gets a full minute of song. Better for adult-skewing weddings.

**Song choices that work:**
- *Marry You* (Bruno Mars) — joyful, instantly recognizable
- *Signed Sealed Delivered* (Stevie Wonder) — never dates
- *Crazy in Love* (Beyoncé) — if you want grown-and-sexy
- *Pharaon* (Gipsy Kings) — Latin-inflected, dramatic
- A song from your wedding country / culture — almost always the right call

### MOMENT 02 — FIRST DANCE (2.5–4 minutes)

The most photographed dance of your life. Three planning questions:

**1. Is the full song too long?** Most modern pop songs are 3:30–4:00. After 2:30, the room starts watching their phones. Edit down to 2:30–3:00 with your musician or DJ.

**2. Will it be a live arrangement or a recording?** A live guitar (or pianist) version of your first-dance song is often more emotional than the recording, because it's played *just for you, in this room, this once.* The recording is what you'll hear on the radio for the rest of your life.

**3. Do you need lessons?** If the answer is "we'll figure it out" — book two hours with a wedding-dance instructor. $200, prevents 100% of the awkward sway-and-pivot first dances that haunt wedding videos.

---

## PAGE 16 · RECEPTION — PARENT DANCES, TOASTS, DINNER

### MOMENT 03 — PARENT DANCES (4–8 minutes total)

Traditionally: father-daughter dance, then mother-son dance.
Modernly: *any combination that fits your family.*

**Common alternatives:**
- Stepparent dances (combining biological + step-parent into a single dance)
- Memorial dances (a song played in honor of a parent who's passed)
- Skipping parent dances entirely if the family dynamic calls for it
- Combined parents-only dance (less awkward when family relationships are complicated)

**The 90-second rule:** parent dances feel longest at the 2:30 mark. Edit to 90 seconds — 2 minutes max. Your parents will thank you.

### MOMENT 04 — TOASTS (15–25 minutes)

**The music question nobody asks:** what plays *under* toasts?

Most weddings get this wrong. Either there's no music (and the room feels sterile), or the music is too loud (and the speaker can't be heard). The right answer:

- **Very soft instrumental bed** — solo guitar, solo piano, or a classical recording
- **Volume just below conversation level** — present but not noticed
- **Fades out completely** when each toaster begins, fades back in during pauses or transitions

A live musician can do this by feel. A DJ has to plan it. Either way, *somebody* needs to be assigned to the music during toasts. If you skip this, the toasts will feel awkward — and your DJ will start every speech with an abrupt music cut.

### MOMENT 05 — DINNER (45–75 minutes)

The longest sustained music block of the night. The hardest one to get right.

**The goal:** music that's present, on-vibe, and never asks anything of the room. Guests should be deep in conversation. Music should not interrupt that.

**Best music for dinner:**
- A live musician (guitar, piano, jazz trio) playing the same kinds of pieces from cocktail hour but tuned 10% softer
- A curated playlist by your DJ — *not* the dance-floor warm-up

**Worst music for dinner:**
- Anything with strong vocals (Adele will make eight tables stop talking)
- Anything fast or rhythmic (creates restlessness)
- Silence (kills the energy)

## PAGE 17 · RECEPTION — THE DANCE FLOOR OPENING

### MOMENT 06 — DANCE FLOOR OPENING (the most important transition of the night)

This is the single moment that determines whether your reception is *the wedding everyone talks about* or *the wedding where the dance floor stayed empty until midnight.*

**The transition has three phases:**

**Phase 1 — The bridge song (3–4 minutes).** Played while dessert finishes and guests sense the pivot. A song with rising energy but no dancing imperative yet. *Can't Take My Eyes Off You* (Frankie Valli). *Brown Eyed Girl* (Van Morrison). *September* (Earth Wind & Fire) — modern classic that pulls guests up without a request.

**Phase 2 — The "open the floor" song (4 minutes).** The first song the DJ plays *for dancing.* This song must:
- Be instantly recognizable (no one risks the first dance on a song they don't know)
- Be familiar across generations (grandparents AND cousins recognize it)
- Be at a tempo that suits all ages (somewhere between 110–130 BPM)
- Have a *very fast hook* — guests are watching for the moment when the song "starts"

**Classic openers that work every time:**
- *Uptown Funk* (Bruno Mars / Mark Ronson)
- *Shut Up and Dance* (Walk the Moon)
- *I Wanna Dance with Somebody* (Whitney Houston)
- *La Bicicleta* (Shakira / Carlos Vives) — for Miami / Latin crowds

**Phase 3 — Sustain (15–25 minutes).** The DJ now has a critical 20-minute window to keep the floor full. The rule: *no song below dance tempo for the first 5 songs.* If the floor empties early, it's twice as hard to refill.

**Sigano Music tip:** brief your DJ to *not take requests for the first 30 minutes* of the dance floor. This is the only time of the night when an audience-pleasing strategy backfires. The DJ knows what opens a floor. Let them work.

---

## PAGE 18 · RECEPTION — TOASTS THROUGH LAST DANCE

### MOMENT 07 — LAST DANCE (3–5 minutes)

The song everyone will remember the wedding by.

**Three candidates that always work:**

**Option A — The romantic close.** A slow song. Couples dance with their partners. Singles dance with friends. *At Last* (Etta James). *Wonderful Tonight* (Eric Clapton). *Make You Feel My Love* (Adele or Dylan).

**Option B — The high-energy close.** Send everyone out at peak. *(I've Had) The Time of My Life* (Bill Medley & Jennifer Warnes). *Don't Stop Believin'* (Journey). *Closing Time* (Semisonic).

**Option C — The cultural close.** A song that means something to your family, your culture, your love story. The hardest to get right, the most memorable when you do.

**The Sigano Music take:** Option B almost always beats Option A for the *guest experience.* You'll have your romantic slow dance moments throughout the night. The last dance is your gift to the people who came to celebrate you. Let them leave on a peak.

## PAGE 20 · PART IV — LOGISTICS

### THE "DO-NOT-PLAY" LIST

The most overlooked tool in wedding music planning. *Far* more important than the must-play list.

**Why it matters:** every musician and every DJ has go-to crowd-pleasers they'll reach for if guests start requesting them. Without an explicit do-not-play list, you're delegating those decisions to your vendor. With one, you're protecting your wedding from the songs that would make you cringe in the video years later.

**Common do-not-play categories:**
- The exes' song (*you know the one*)
- Anything that played at your divorce or your parents' divorce
- Cheesy line dances (*Macarena*, *Cha Cha Slide*, *Cupid Shuffle*) — exclude them by name
- Specific artists you don't want represented
- Religious songs that don't fit your wedding (e.g. *Ave Maria* at a non-religious ceremony)
- Anything political

**The format:**

Give your musician/DJ a written list, 10–25 entries, with the song *title and artist* (some songs have many covers). One line per entry. Signed off by both partners.

### OUTDOOR VS. INDOOR SOUND

**Outdoor weddings need 30–50% more PA than indoor weddings of the same size.** Sound dissipates over open ground. If you're getting married at Vizcaya gardens or a beach venue, ask your musician/DJ specifically:

- *How many speakers will you bring for an outdoor event of [X] guests?*
- *Do you bring battery-powered options if power access is limited?*
- *What's the backup plan if a speaker fails?*

A musician who hesitates on these questions hasn't done many outdoor luxury weddings.

### WEATHER BACKUP

For outdoor ceremonies, the music plan must include:

- A covered backup location for the musician (a guitarist's classical guitar is a $4,000 instrument that is destroyed by rain)
- A plan for power if the backup location is different from the primary
- A 30-minute lead time on the rain call (the venue calls it, the musician moves)

## PAGE 21 · PART IV — LOGISTICS (continued)

### COORDINATING MULTIPLE VENDORS

The vendors who need to talk to each other before the wedding:

**Planner ↔ Musician** — the planner controls the timeline. The musician needs to know it down to the minute. *Get this introduction handled six weeks before the wedding, not the week of.*

**Musician ↔ Photographer / Videographer** — for first-dance / parent-dance music, the music team and the camera team need to coordinate cues. Bad weddings have the first dance start before the photographer is in position.

**Musician ↔ Venue AV** — for indoor receptions especially, the musician needs to confirm power, monitor positioning, and any house PA the venue requires they patch into.

**Musician ↔ Officiant** — for the ceremony, the officiant needs a wireless lapel mic. Your musician's PA usually supplies one. If you're using a separate AV vendor for ceremony sound, brief both teams.

### THE TECH RIDER

A professional musician will send a **one-page tech rider** to your planner about two weeks before the wedding. It lists:

- Setup time required (typically 60–90 minutes)
- Power needs (most need one or two 110V outlets)
- Stage / placement requirements (covered area, level surface, dimensions)
- Sound check window (45 minutes before guest arrival)
- Vendor meal (yes or no — pro vendors usually skip)
- Break schedule
- End-time and overtime rates

If your musician doesn't send a tech rider unprompted, ask for one. Its absence is a yellow flag.

## PAGE 22 · PART V — HIRING A MUSICIAN

# 12 Questions to Ask Before You Book

Copy this list. Use it on every musician you interview. Their answers tell you more than their highlight reel does.

**1. How many weddings have you performed in the last 12 months?**
*Looking for: at least 25 for a full-time wedding musician. Below 10 means you're paying for someone's side hustle.*

**2. Have you played at our specific venue before?**
*If yes, ask for the date. If no, ask if they'll do a site visit at no charge.*

**3. Can you send me a full unedited performance video — not just your highlight reel?**
*Highlight reels hide what musicians sound like over 4 hours. Full footage doesn't.*

**4. Do you provide your own PA, mics, and backup gear?**
*The correct answer is yes, and they should have backup for every critical piece. "I can rent it" is a red flag.*

**5. Do you carry liability insurance? Can you provide a Certificate of Insurance to our venue?**
*Most luxury Miami venues require $1M–$2M general liability with the venue named as additionally insured. If they can't provide a COI within 24 hours, they aren't insured.*

**6. What does your contract include?**
*Specifically: hours of performance, song requests, custom arrangements, travel, meals, overtime rates, and cancellation policy.*

---

## PAGE 23 · PART V — HIRING A MUSICIAN (continued)

**7. What's your contingency plan if you're sick on the wedding day?**
*Pro musicians have a stable of trusted replacements. "I never get sick" is not an answer.*

**8. Will you prepare custom arrangements of our requested songs at no extra charge?**
*Yes is the answer you want. If they charge extra, the rate should be modest ($50–150 per arrangement).*

**9. What's your typical timeline from booking to wedding day?**
*Looking for: initial planning call within 2 weeks, song-list lock 60 days out, final timeline review 14 days out.*

**10. How do you handle the ceremony-to-cocktail-hour transition if I hire you for both?**
*A great musician has a specific answer: covered bridge music, simultaneous setup, no awkward silence. Vague answers = guaranteed awkward silence on the day.*

**11. Can I see your reviews on The Knot, WeddingWire, and Google?**
*Verify the reviews independently. Fake reviews exist. Real reviews mention specific dates and venues.*

**12. What's your retainer, balance schedule, and refund policy?**
*Industry standard: 30% non-refundable retainer at booking, 70% balance due 30 days before the wedding. Refund policies vary — get them in writing.*

### THREE RED FLAGS

- **They don't have a contract.** Walk away.
- **They quote prices verbally and change them after you commit.** Walk away.
- **They badmouth other musicians or vendors in your meeting.** Walk away. The wedding industry is small and collaborative — bad-mouthing means you don't know how to play with others.
